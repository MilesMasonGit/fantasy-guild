# The Rules Line — concept

*Written 2026-09-09, after an owner interview. The authoritative plan with its
locked decisions is [`rules_line_editor_roadmap.md`](rules_line_editor_roadmap.md).*

## The one-sentence version

**A rule is a line of prose you write into.** Not a form that produces prose, and
not prose that a parser turns into a rule — the sentence on screen *is* the rule,
and every word in it that represents a decision is a word you can click and
retype.

## Why this, and why now

The grammar rework (Effects Grammar v2) gave the game one vocabulary for every
rule: verbs, roles, filters, magnitudes, moments, live effect instances. It works.
The vocabulary is large and about to get larger.

The editor did not keep up, and it failed in a specific and instructive way.

V3 built a chip row — decisions rendered as small controls, read left to right,
narrowing as you type — and it was the right idea. But it was **added on top of
the old forms rather than replacing them**. Four pickers were deleted; fourteen
form components stayed. So today a `Deals` rule shows you a "damage" chip and an
"ignores armour" chip in the row, and then a *"Damage"* number box and an
*"Ignores armour"* checkbox in a form directly beneath it. The same two decisions,
twice, in two idioms, on one screen.

The owner's verdict on the current screen, and the three things wrong with it:

* the duplicate forms below,
* a popover per chip — *"that is still a dropdown, just a smaller one"*,
* too dense to read as a sentence at all.

Notably **not** wrong: you can still see what the rule says while you change it.
The rendered sentence underneath has been doing its job. That is the part to
build on.

## The idea

Today the screen shows you a row of controls, and *underneath* it, the sentence
those controls produce. You author in one place and read in another.

**Collapse them.** There is one line. It reads as the finished sentence, because
it *is* the finished sentence — the same `renderStatement` output the Token
inspector and the game's own rules text use. The difference is that the words
standing for decisions are live: put the cursor on one and type, and it becomes a
different word, chosen from what is legal exactly there.

> When this Token's own cycle completes, deals **2** damage to **the actor**.

Click `2`, type `5`. Click `the actor`, type "adj" and the panel narrows to
*adjacent Tokens*, *adjacent heroes*, *the source*. Nothing else in the sentence
moves.

## What this is not

**It is not a parser.** You never type a sentence and have software work out what
you meant. Every word you commit is a word the system offered you, so the editor
can never hold something it does not understand, there is no error recovery, and
there is no "what did it read that as?" to debug. That guarantee — an invalid
rule stays unwritable — is the thing the old dropdowns bought and the thing this
must not spend.

**It is not a second renderer.** The line is generated by the same function that
prints a rule everywhere else in the game. This matters more than it sounds: the
owner's standing rule is that a rule which reads badly means *the syntax should
improve*, and because the editor is the renderer's output, improving the wording
improves the tool in the same edit. Two renderers would mean the editor and the
game could quietly disagree about what a rule says.

## What has to be true for it to work

1. **The renderer must be able to say which decision each word came from.** It
   builds a string today. It needs to build an annotated sequence, of which the
   string is just the joined form.
2. **Every decision must be sayable in the line.** Any decision that still needs
   a form beneath is a decision the sentence cannot express — and every one of
   those is a small hole the old maze grows back through.
3. **The vocabulary must stay browsable.** Typing is not self-documenting. A
   dropdown at least shows you what exists; a text field shows you nothing. The
   panel beside the line, listing everything legal at the cursor with its hint, is
   not a convenience — it is the only way an author meets a word they did not
   know the game had.

## What deliberately stays out of the line

The sentence carries what a rule **does**. What it **costs** and how often it may
fire — charge cost, cooldown, upkeep, chance, the `requires` clause — sit in a
quiet panel beside it. They are a different kind of decision, they are changed far
less often, and folding them in would put four numbers in the way of every
sentence you read.

## The measure of success

The owner will design and tune many effects over the life of this project, and
will **tweak far more often than create**. So the test is not how fast a new rule
can be written from nothing. It is:

* changing one word of an existing rule costs one click and a few keystrokes,
* a word you do not know exists is findable without leaving the line,
* and reading a rule back tells you exactly what the game will do.
